The problem

People couldn't figure out how to buy a ticket, find an event, or plan a basic visit. The navigation was broken and the ticket flow just didn't go anywhere.

What we did

We rebuilt the three pages people struggled with most: home, tickets and events, based on a heuristic evaluation, an accessibility audit, surveys and usability tests.

01

Overview

Planning a visit shouldn't feel this hard

azscience.org is how most people plan their visit to the Arizona Science Center. The three of us ran a full UX audit and rebuilt the pages where people kept getting stuck, so the site would finally be clear enough to just scan and go.

02

Heuristic evaluation

Where the site breaks down

Each of us went through the site on our own using Nielsen's heuristics, and we flagged the exact same critical issues at the exact same severity. That's when we knew the problems weren't just our opinion.

Critical

“Buy Tickets” leads nowhere

The nav item takes users to a page with no way to purchase. Exhibition selections redirect without explanation.

Critical

No guidance after redirects

During ticket purchasing, users are never told what's happening, so they get lost mid-flow.

Major

Two navigation systems

Two competing nav bars behave differently on every page, breaking consistency and predictability.

Major

Events are buried

Event content is hard to find and scan, even though users ranked events as a top priority.

03

User research

What people told us

Between the survey and nine moderated usability sessions, we kept hearing the same story: people were curious to explore, but friction wore them down, and nobody walked away feeling confident.

“I couldn't figure out how to actually buy a ticket. I kept getting sent to pages that didn't let me purchase anything.”

Survey participant · Ticket flow

“I wanted to see what events were happening, but I couldn't find them without digging through the whole site.”

Survey participant · Event discovery

Every participant hit friction trying to buy a ticket, and not one of them felt confident afterward.

Research synthesis · 27 surveys + 9 usability tests
04

Accessibility audit

One profile at a time isn't enough

We ran WAVE and WCAG-informed manual checks. The site does have an accessibility menu, but it only lets you pick one profile at a time.

People's needs overlap. Forcing one choice locks out the exact people the feature was built for.

06

Usability testing

Completion hid the friction

All nine people finished their tasks, but almost every session hit the same walls: scrolling to the footer for basic info, clicking sidebar items that did nothing, or being sent through a ticket flow with zero explanation.

Finishing a task didn't mean the experience worked. People admitted far more uncertainty afterward than their think-aloud let on.

07

Redesign

Three pages, rebuilt from research

Every change came straight out of what we found in testing: a clearer hierarchy, fewer dead ends, and a next step that's always obvious.

HomepageOne stable navigation, direct ticket access, and content you can scan.
TicketsFour clear steps (date, admission, extras, review) with a persistent cart.
EventsFeatured cards, top-level filters and visible calls to action.

Reflection

What this taught me

Designing from research means trusting what people did over what they said afterward. The real finding wasn't in the survey answers; it was in the gap between finishing a task and still feeling unsure. Completion rates alone would never have shown us that.
